**ROBERT MCKEE'S CHARACTER ARCHITECTURE (Story, Chapter 6: "Structure and Character"):**

- **TRUE CHARACTER VS CHARACTERIZATION**: Marcus's characterization includes being a former priest who drinks whiskey. However, his true character is revealed in his choice under pressure - he takes justice into his own hands by killing a man who confessed to beating three children to death (McKee, p.101).
- **CHARACTER ARC**: Marcus begins believing in divine justice but ends up taking justice into his own hands, embodying McKee's principle of profound change from start to end (McKee, p.104-106). Applying McKee's formula: Pressure (the man confessing the murders) + Choice (administering poisoned communion wine) = True Character (man who kills for justice).
- **DIMENSION THROUGH CONTRADICTION**: Marcus, a former priest, committing murder creates an internal opposition, making him dimensional according to McKee's principle of contradiction within deep character (McKee, p.107).

**JOHN TRUBY'S CHARACTER WEB (Anatomy of Story, Chapter 3: "The Seven Key Steps of Story Structure"):**

- **WEAKNESS/NEED**: Marcus's moral weakness is playing God, and his psychological need is to accept the limits of human justice (Truby p.43).
- **GHOST**: The loss of faith can be seen as Marcus's ghost, driving his present actions (Truby p.46).
- **DESIRE LINE**: Marcus desires justice, which serves as the spine of the story (Truby p.48-50).
- **SELF-REVELATION**: Elena's warning could push Marcus toward a moment of self-revelation regarding his actions and their consequences (Truby, p.51).

**SYD FIELD'S THREE-DIMENSIONAL CHARACTER (Screenplay, Chapter 4: "Building a Character"):**

- **PROFESSIONAL/PERSONAL/PRIVATE**: Marcus's professional life as a former priest is contrasted with his personal relationship with Elena and his private guilt and use of whiskey to cope (Field p.46-48).
- **CHARACTER BIOGRAPHY**: The script informs us that Marcus lost faith once, creating an intriguing backstory element influencing present behavior (Field p.49-51).

**BLAKE SNYDER'S TRANSFORMATION MACHINE (Save the Cat, Throughout):**

- **SAVE THE CAT MOMENT**: Marcus showing empathy for murdered children is an early moment that reveals his moral center and prompts audience connection (Snyder p.12-14).
- **SHARD OF GLASS**: The phrase "You're not God" represents a thematic shard of glass, highlighting Marcus's character problem (Snyder p.72).
- **TRANSFORMATION MACHINE**: The script transforms Marcus from faith to action, embodying Snyder's belief that movies are about transformation (Snyder p.134-136).

**CHRISTOPHER VOGLER'S ARCHETYPES (Writer's Journey, Chapter on Archetypes):**

- **HERO ARCHETYPE**: Marcus is a flawed hero with a tragic flaw that humanizes him (Vogler p.30-35).
- **SHADOW ARCHETYPE**: The killer serves as an external shadow, representing the darker aspects of humanity, and also mirrors Marcus's internal shadow - his own dark impulses (Vogler p.65-70).
- **SHAPESHIFTER**: Elena shifts from ally to opposition throughout the scene, embodying Vogler's "character changing allegiance" concept (Vogler p.71-76).

**JOSEPH CAMPBELL'S HERO PATTERN (Hero with a Thousand Faces):**

- **SUPERNATURAL AID REFUSED**: Marcus rejecting divine justice by taking matters into his own hands embodies Campbell's "hero refusing aid" concept (Campbell p.72-77).
- **APOTHEOSIS**: Realizing he's become what he opposed represents a moment of divine knowledge for Marcus, reflecting Campbell's "divine knowledge moment" (Campbell p.148-151).

**ARISTOTLE'S TRAGIC HERO (Poetics, Chapter 13):**

- **HAMARTIA**: Marcus's pride is the hamartia causing his downfall as a tragic hero (Aristotle 1453a7-10).
- **ETHOS**: As a former priest, Marcus embodies Aristotle's concept of a good person with a flaw (Aristotle 1450b8-10).
