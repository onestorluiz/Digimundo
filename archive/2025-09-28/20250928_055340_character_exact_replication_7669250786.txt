**ARISTOTLE'S POETICS:**

Aristotle posits that tragedy, through character development, arouses pity and fear, which leads to catharsis. Marcus's hamartia, or error in judgment, is giving the Morrison boy communion wine laced with poison as an instrument of justice for his heinous sins. This decision stems from his deep-seated character flaw - a struggle between his religious convictions and moral obligation towards innocent lives.

Quoting Aristotle, "The change to bad fortune which he undergoes is not due to any depravity, but to some error of judgment" (Poetics). Marcus's tragic flaw isn't viciousness but rather misguided zeal. Elena serves as the catalyst for his recognition and reversal when she confronts him about his actions.

The relationship between character and plot development is evident in that Marcus's tragic decision propels the narrative forward, shaping its outcome significantly. 

**ROBERT MCKEE'S STORY:**

McKee emphasizes that true character is revealed under pressure. Marcus's former faith and current inner turmoil are exposed through his actions, especially when faced with moral dilemmas concerning justice and forgiveness. He embodies McKee's notion of a character arc, transitioning from a priest seeking absolution for his act of vengeance to accepting responsibility for his decisions.

Marcus's complexity lies in his struggle between his past as a man of God and present moral ambiguity, reflecting McKee's assertion that "Story is about human change." The dynamic between Marcus and Elena showcases the importance of character dimension, as they both grapple with their shared religious background while navigating vastly different paths.

**JOHN TRUBY'S ANATOMY OF STORY:**

Truby posits that a well-crafted story must have a ghost, need, and desire progression in its characters. Marcus's 'ghost' is his inability to reconcile divine justice with earthly retribution. His 'need' is to absolve himself of his sins, while his 'desire' is to serve justice where he perceives it lacking within the system.

Moreover, Truby argues for a character web and relationship dynamics. Elena, as Marcus's confidante, plays a pivotal role in challenging his perspective on their shared oath of preserving life versus seeking justice.

**SYD FIELD'S SCREENPLAY:**

Field's three-act structure is evident in this script. In Act One, we see Marcus's backstory, motivation, and objective - to deal with the guilt of his actions against the Morrison boy's father. His growth and transformation occur throughout the story, climaxing in his decision to accept responsibility for his deeds.

**CHRISTOPHER VOGLER'S WRITER'S JOURNEY:**

Vogler's monomyth applies to Marcus's character journey. He embarks on a separation from his past identity as a priest, undergoes initiation via the moral dilemma posed by the Morrison boy's father, and returns with newfound wisdom about the complexities of justice and forgiveness.

**BLAKE SNYDER'S SAVE THE CAT:**

Marcus is introduced as a former priest grappling with a moral quandary, which instantly makes him both intriguing and sympathetic, satisfying Save the Cat's requirement for likable protagonists. His transformation unfolds through various beats, ultimately leading to his redemption arc completion.

**JOSEPH CAMPBELL'S HERO WITH A THOUSAND FACES:**

Campbell's archetypal hero cycle is evident in Marcus's story. He faces a call to action (confronting the Morrison boy's father), refuses it due to internal conflict, receives aid from Elena, undergoes trials and tribulations, and finally achieves a form of self-discovery and acceptance.

By integrating these theories, we can comprehensively analyze Marcus's character development, revealing his struggles with faith, justice, and forgiveness. Despite minor contradictions among different approaches, synthesizing them paints a rich tapestry of Marcus's inner turmoil and growth.
