 STORY DESIGN ANALYSIS:
McKee states, "Pacing is controlled through the design of scenes and sequence" (McKee, 1997, p. 203). In this script, each scene's turning point contributes to the overall rhythm. The first scene establishes a mundane routine with Sarah's dialogue indicating her ennui. This initial tranquility is shattered by the phone call, marking the inciting incident and altering the story's direction.

McKee explains that a scene must have a "value charge" which can be positive, negative, or neutral (McKee, 1997, p. 210). Here, we see a clear shift from negative (boring office life) to increasingly positive values (escalating danger and urgency), culminating in the climactic sequence of CEO abduction.

PROGRESSIVE COMPLICATIONS:
Each scene turns the story in a new direction as complications accelerate the tempo. McKee's Law of Diminishing Returns applies here; each explosion intensifies, increasing tension and stakes. The first explosion startles; the second prompts panic; the third leads to evacuation. This escalating pattern of disruption propels the narrative forward.

CRISIS AND CLIMAX PACING:
At the crisis moment, Sarah is frozen at her desk while her office is under attack. McKee notes, "The crisis is the final test of character" (McKee, 1997, p. 234). Here, it tests Sarah's courage and presence of mind. Her call to 911 signifies her resolve to act despite fear.

Regarding climax timing, McKee distinguishes between the 'climactic moment' and the 'final climax'. In this script, the climactic moment occurs when Sarah decides to report the incident, while the final climax is the abduction itself - the culmination of the sequence's rising action.

SCENE ANALYSIS METHOD:
Let's break down the scene beats. Scene one begins with a neutral value charge (routine office work), shifts to negative (boredom) with Sarah's dialogue, then positive (curiosity) upon receiving the call. The subtext of the mysterious phone call implies danger, disrupting the rhythm and raising stakes.

In scene two, fear takes over as the building shakes from the explosion. Here, McKee's principle of value change is evident again: negative (fear) turns to increasingly positive values (escalating panic and urgency). Subtext plays a crucial role; although characters verbally express panic, their actions reveal deeper fears and motivations.
