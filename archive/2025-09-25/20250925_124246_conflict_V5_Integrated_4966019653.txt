1. McKee's Techniques:
   - The Gap (p.142-144): Marcus expected redemption and forgiveness from his religious duties, but the reality is that he feels guilt and unworthiness due to his actions.
   - Three Levels of Conflict (p.210-220): Intrapersonal conflict - Marcus' internal struggle between his faith and morality; Interpersonal conflict - tension between Marcus and Elena due to their differing views on justice; Institutional conflict - the larger conflict within the religious institution they both used to be a part of.
   - True character revealed in choices under pressure (p.101): Marcus' choice to poison the confessed murderer shows his prioritization of justice over religious teachings, revealing his true character.
   - Progressive Complications (p.211): The complications escalate as Marcus and Elena's conflict deepens with their contrasting perspectives on justice and morality.

2. Truby's Techniques:
   - Four-Corner Opposition (p.88-95): Marcus, a former priest, is in opposition to the murderer who confessed to him; Elena, a nurse and ex-nun, serves as another angle of opposition due to her moral stance on preserving life.
   - Moral Argument through conflict (p.118): The script presents a moral argument regarding justice and morality through Marcus' actions and the ensuing conflict with Elena.
   - Web of Conflict (p.90): A complex web of conflicts arises from Marcus' decision, involving his faith, personal sense of justice, Elena's beliefs, and religious institutions.
   - The opponent defines the hero (p.91): Elena serves as a foil to Marcus, challenging his views on justice and morality, thus defining him as a character who questions religious dogmas.

3. Field's Techniques:
   - Plot Point I (p.26): Marcus' decision to poison the confessed murderer sets the story in motion.
   - Midpoint (p.113): Elena entering and discovering the truth about the Morrison boy serves as a turning point, shifting the dynamics of conflict.
   - Three-Act Structure (p.21): The script follows this structure with Marcus' decision being Act One, their confrontation constituting Act Two, and Elena's plea serving as Act Three.
   - Drama is conflict (p.25): The entire narrative revolves around the conflicts arising from Marcus' choice to administer poisoned communion wine.

4. Snyder's Techniques:
   - Catalyst (p.76): The confession of the Morrison boy's murder serves as a catalyst for the story, leading to further complications and conflict.
   - All Is Lost (p.86): Marcus experiences an emotional "All Is Lost" moment when Elena confronts him about his actions and their oath to preserve life.
   - Bad Guys Close In (p.83): The moral conflict closes in on Marcus as he grapples with the consequences of his decision and Elena's increasing disapproval.
   - The finale proves the thesis (p.90): The script ultimately demonstrates that personal morality can clash with religious institutions, questioning if justice transcends faith.

5. Vogler's Techniques:
   - Shadow (p.71): Marcus' inner conflict serves as a metaphorical shadow, personifying the darker side of his beliefs and moral code.
   - Threshold Guardian (p.57): Elena plays the role of a threshold guardian by challenging Marcus' views on justice and morality, forcing him to confront his choices.
   - Ordeal (p.155): The emotional ordeal occurs when Marcus is faced with the reality of his actions and their consequences.
   - Every hero needs a worthy opponent (p.72): Elena serves as a worthy opponent for Marcus by offering an alternative perspective on faith, justice, and morality.

6. Campbell's Techniques:
   - Dragon Battle (p.108): The ultimate conflict lies within Marcus himself as he battles his internal struggles between faith and personal morals.
   - Atonement with Father (p.126): Marcus' relationship with the religious institution can be viewed as an "authority conflict" similar to the archetype described by Campbell.
   - Belly of the Whale (p.90): Marcus experiences a metaphorical journey through his internal struggle, representing a descent into the unknown.
   - Where we thought to slay another, we shall slay ourselves (p.136): This quote resonates with Marcus' story as he ultimately grapples with the consequences of his actions and their impact on himself.

7. Aristotle's Techniques:
   - Hamartia (1453a): Marcus' tragic flaw is his willingness to take justice into his own hands, leading to internal and interpersonal conflicts.
   - Peripeteia (1452a): The reversal of fortune occurs when Marcus realizes the gravity of his actions and faces Elena's reproach.
   - Agon (1448b): The narrative presents a dramatic contest between Marcus' moral judgment and religious teachings, which shapes his character growth.
   - Character revealed through conflict (1450b): The script highlights how Marcus' true character is unveiled through the conflicts that arise from his decision to administer poisoned communion wine.
